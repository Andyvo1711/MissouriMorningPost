For residents of several small Missouri towns, a video screen has become the fastest route to a specialist's opinion. Mercy has expanded its telehealth network into a wider swath of rural counties this winter, adding virtual visit stations at community clinics and rural health outposts that previously had little more than a nurse on staff a few days a week.

The expansion builds on a telehealth infrastructure Mercy has developed over more than a decade, originally centered on connecting rural emergency rooms with critical care physicians in real time. The newer phase focuses on routine and follow-up care, letting patients see cardiologists, endocrinologists, and behavioral health providers from an exam room in their own county rather than driving two or three hours to a larger Mercy facility.

## Closing the Distance Gap

Missouri's rural hospitals have faced persistent workforce shortages, particularly in specialty fields, making it difficult for smaller communities to retain physicians beyond primary care and general practice. Telehealth has increasingly become a stopgap, allowing local clinics to keep patients closer to home for conditions that would otherwise require travel to St. Louis, Springfield, or Kansas City.

"A lot of our patients are managing chronic conditions like diabetes or heart failure, and consistency matters more than almost anything else," said a Mercy clinic administrator involved in the rollout. "If someone has to choose between missing a half day of work to drive two hours for a fifteen-minute appointment or skipping the visit altogether, too often they skip it. Telehealth removes that choice."

Clinic staff say the technology itself has become simpler to use over the past few years, with high-definition cameras and diagnostic peripherals, including digital stethoscopes and otoscopes, that let a local nurse gather clinical information a remote physician can review in real time. That combination allows for a level of examination that older video-only systems could not support.

## Mental Health Access a Priority

Behavioral health has emerged as one of the fastest-growing uses of the expanded network. Rural Missouri counties have historically had few licensed therapists or psychiatrists, and Mercy officials say telehealth appointments for counseling and psychiatric medication management have grown substantially since being added to the rural clinic menu. Patients can schedule recurring sessions without the added stress of travel, which providers say has improved appointment consistency.

A licensed clinical social worker who now sees several rural patients through the program described the shift as significant for people who might otherwise go without care entirely. "Some of these patients have never had a therapist within driving distance," she said. "Being able to log on from the same clinic where they get their blood pressure checked normalizes the idea that mental health is just part of health."

## Remaining Challenges

Despite the expansion, gaps remain. Broadband connectivity is inconsistent in parts of rural Missouri, and some patients still struggle to get a reliable video connection from home, which is why Mercy has prioritized clinic-based telehealth stations over at-home visits in its most remote service areas. Hospital officials also note that telehealth cannot fully replace hands-on care for surgical follow-up or conditions requiring imaging equipment not available locally.

Even so, county health officials in several of the newly added service areas say the expansion represents meaningful progress. Local leaders have pointed to reduced emergency room usage for non-emergency specialty needs as an early sign the model is working, though they caution that longer-term data will be needed to fully assess its impact on health outcomes across the state's rural communities.
