Along stretches of rural Missouri, the local hospital often functions as more than a place to receive care. It is frequently among the largest employers in the county, a training ground for local nursing graduates, and for many residents, the only realistic option in a medical emergency. That role has come under increasing financial strain in recent years, and several small hospitals across the state are now weighing which services they can continue to sustain.

Rural hospital administrators point to a familiar combination of pressures: declining and aging populations that shrink the local patient base, higher rates of uninsured and underinsured patients, difficulty recruiting physicians willing to practice outside metro areas, and reimbursement rates that often fail to cover the true cost of care in low-volume settings. Several facilities have already scaled back obstetric services or inpatient beds in recent years rather than close entirely.

## A Difficult Balancing Act

"Closing isn't usually the first step, it's often the last one after years of cutting back," said a rural hospital administrator whose facility has reduced services but remains open. "We've had to make choices about which departments we can staff safely and which ones we simply can't sustain with the volume and reimbursement we're working with."

Obstetric care has been one of the most visible casualties of this pressure nationally, and Missouri has not been immune. Maintaining a labor and delivery unit requires round-the-clock staffing by specially trained nurses and access to physicians capable of managing complications, a cost structure that becomes difficult to justify in counties where only a handful of births occur each month. When rural obstetric units close, expectant mothers are often left driving well over an hour to the nearest hospital still delivering babies, a shift that public health researchers have linked to worse outcomes in some cases.

## Workforce Shortages Compound the Problem

Recruiting physicians and specialists to rural Missouri has grown more difficult as newer generations of doctors increasingly prefer urban and suburban practice settings, drawn by higher pay, more manageable call schedules, and proximity to specialty colleagues. Rural hospitals have responded with loan repayment incentives, partnerships with medical schools for rural rotations, and expanded reliance on nurse practitioners and physician assistants to fill gaps left by physician shortages.

A county health department director in one affected community said the workforce challenge extends beyond doctors. "It's not just physicians, it's radiology techs, respiratory therapists, pharmacists. Every specialized role is harder to fill out here, and larger health systems can simply offer more."

## Efforts to Adapt

Some rural hospitals have pursued partnerships with larger health systems, gaining access to shared administrative resources, telehealth specialist support, and group purchasing power for supplies and equipment, in exchange for operating under a larger system's umbrella. Others have restructured into smaller designated facilities that focus on emergency stabilization and outpatient services, transferring more complex cases to larger hospitals rather than attempting to maintain full inpatient capability.

State health officials say they are monitoring the situation closely, aware that each rural hospital closure or service reduction can ripple through a community well beyond healthcare, affecting local employment, emergency response times, and residents' willingness to remain in or move to a given county. Advocacy groups have called for stronger reimbursement support for rural facilities, though any significant policy shift would likely require action at both the state and federal level. For now, many rural Missouri hospitals continue to operate on thin margins, adapting service by service to remain open for the communities that depend on them.
