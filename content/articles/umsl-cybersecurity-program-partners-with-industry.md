The University of Missouri-St. Louis is broadening its cybersecurity program's connections with regional employers, adding new internship pathways and guest-taught modules meant to keep coursework aligned with the fast-moving demands of the cybersecurity job market.

Program leaders say the effort responds to steady demand from students who want clearer pathways into cybersecurity careers, as well as from area employers who have struggled to fill technical security roles. St. Louis has become home to a growing cluster of technology and financial services firms with dedicated security operations teams, and university officials see an opportunity to position UMSL graduates as a pipeline for those roles.

## Building Hands-On Experience

As part of the expanded partnerships, several regional companies have agreed to offer structured internships specifically for cybersecurity students, along with occasional guest lectures from working security professionals who walk students through real-world incident response scenarios. Faculty say exposing students to actual case studies, rather than purely theoretical coursework, has become an increasingly important part of preparing graduates for entry-level security analyst roles.

"Cybersecurity is one of those fields where the tools and threats change so quickly that textbooks alone can't keep up," said a faculty member who helps coordinate the program's industry partnerships. "Having practitioners come in and talk through what they're actually seeing on the job makes a real difference for our students."

The program has also expanded access to a dedicated computer lab equipped for hands-on exercises, including simulated network intrusion scenarios that let students practice defensive techniques without risking real systems. Enrollment in the university's cybersecurity coursework has grown steadily over the past several years, a trend administrators attribute both to strong job placement outcomes and to broader public awareness of cybersecurity as a career path.

## Addressing a Regional Talent Gap

Local employers say the partnership expansion comes at a good time. A representative from a St. Louis-based technology trade association noted that security roles have consistently ranked among the hardest positions for local companies to fill, citing both a shortage of qualified applicants and competition from larger firms in other cities offering remote positions.

University officials say they are also working to make the program more accessible to students who might not otherwise consider a cybersecurity track, including offering introductory workshops aimed at students majoring in unrelated fields who want to explore the discipline before committing to it. Several students who transferred into the cybersecurity program from other majors said the flexibility of these entry points was part of what drew them in.

"I started in a completely different major and only found out about this program because a friend mentioned an intro workshop," said one senior now completing an internship with a regional financial firm. "It ended up being the best decision I made in college."

Program administrators say the next phase of the partnership expansion will focus on formalizing a regional advisory board made up of area employers, intended to keep curriculum updates aligned with evolving industry needs on an ongoing basis rather than through occasional, ad hoc adjustments. They expect the advisory board to hold its first meetings later this year.
