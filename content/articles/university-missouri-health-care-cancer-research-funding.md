Inside a research laboratory on the University of Missouri Health Care campus in Columbia, a small team of scientists has spent recent months expanding a study into how cancer treatment outcomes differ for patients living in rural versus urban parts of the state. The project recently received new grant funding that researchers say will allow them to enroll more participants and extend the study's scope over the next several years.

The research centers on a persistent pattern documented in national health data: patients diagnosed with cancer in rural areas often present with more advanced disease at diagnosis and experience worse outcomes than patients in urban centers, even when insurance status and cancer type are similar. University of Missouri researchers are examining what role distance to specialty care, delayed screening, and access to clinical trials play in that gap, with an eye toward developing interventions specific to Missouri's rural communities.

## Bridging a Data Gap

"A lot of the national cancer research is drawn from large urban academic centers, and that data doesn't always translate cleanly to what we see in rural Missouri," said an oncology researcher involved in the project. "We need our own data if we want interventions that actually work for the patients we're treating here."

The funding will support expanded patient recruitment from clinics across mid-Missouri, along with partnerships with smaller regional hospitals that refer complex cancer cases to Columbia. Researchers say building trust with rural patients, some of whom have had limited past interaction with academic medical research, has required deliberate outreach, including partnering with local primary care providers who patients already know and trust.

## Clinical Trials Closer to Home

One focus of the expanded research involves testing whether telehealth-supported clinical trial participation can reduce the burden on rural patients who would otherwise need to travel repeatedly to Columbia for monitoring visits. If successful, the approach could allow patients to remain enrolled in advanced treatment trials without the logistical strain that currently causes some to decline participation altogether.

A clinical trial coordinator working on the project said the travel burden is often underestimated by people outside rural healthcare. "We've had patients who were excellent candidates for a trial that could have significantly extended their life, but they couldn't manage the drive every two weeks, especially during treatment when they're already exhausted," she said. "If we can bring monitoring closer to home, we remove one more barrier."

## What the Funding Supports

Beyond patient recruitment, the grant will fund additional laboratory staff and equipment used to analyze tumor samples for genetic markers that may predict how well a patient will respond to specific therapies. Researchers hope this work will eventually allow oncologists across Missouri, not just in Columbia, to make more precise treatment decisions earlier in a patient's care.

University officials describe the funding as part of a broader institutional commitment to cancer research that serves the specific needs of Missouri's population rather than simply adopting findings developed elsewhere. Administrators note that translating research into everyday clinical practice takes years, but they say the current expansion represents meaningful progress toward a goal that has long been discussed among the university's oncology faculty: closing the outcomes gap between rural and urban cancer patients within the state.
