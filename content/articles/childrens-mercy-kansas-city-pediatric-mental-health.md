Waiting lists for pediatric mental health appointments have grown long enough in the Kansas City area that some families report waiting months for an initial evaluation. In response, Children's Mercy Kansas City has expanded its behavioral health division this spring, adding outpatient counseling capacity and deepening partnerships with area school districts to catch problems earlier.

The expansion reflects a trend hospital administrators describe as impossible to ignore: referrals for anxiety, depression, and related conditions among children and adolescents have climbed steadily in recent years, a pattern echoed in national pediatric health data. Missouri's mental health workforce, like that of many states, has not kept pace with demand, leaving hospitals such as Children's Mercy to absorb much of the overflow.

## Meeting Families Where They Are

Part of the new investment includes embedding behavioral health consultants within several primary care clinics affiliated with the hospital system, allowing pediatricians to bring in a mental health specialist during a regular checkup rather than requiring a separate referral and a second appointment weeks or months later. Hospital officials say this integrated model has already shortened the time between a concern being raised and a child receiving an initial assessment.

"Parents often notice something is wrong long before they know how to get help for it," said a pediatric psychologist who works within the expanded program. "If we can have a conversation the same day a parent mentions it to their pediatrician, instead of sending them off to find a specialist on their own, we catch things earlier, when treatment tends to be more effective."

## Partnering With Schools

The hospital has also increased its work with school counselors across the Kansas City metro area, offering training sessions intended to help school staff recognize early warning signs and connect struggling students with appropriate care. Officials say schools have become an important entry point into the mental health system, since many children spend more waking hours with teachers and counselors than with any single healthcare provider.

A school liaison coordinator involved in the partnership said the goal is not to turn teachers into therapists, but to make sure they know what steps to take when something seems off. "Teachers already notice changes in behavior," she said. "We're just trying to give them a clear, fast pathway so that a concern doesn't sit unaddressed for a whole semester."

## Addressing a Statewide Shortage

Missouri has faced a well-documented shortage of child psychiatrists and licensed counselors trained specifically in pediatric care, with many rural counties having no dedicated child mental health provider at all. Children's Mercy officials acknowledge their expansion cannot single-handedly solve a statewide shortage, but they describe it as an effort to build capacity where they can while also supporting telehealth counseling options for families who live outside the immediate Kansas City area.

Hospital leaders say they are tracking wait times closely as the new positions and school partnerships take effect, with the goal of eventually reducing the interval between referral and first appointment to a matter of weeks rather than months. They caution that recruiting additional child psychiatrists remains difficult given national shortages in the specialty, but say the current expansion represents a meaningful step toward addressing one of the most pressing gaps in pediatric care across the region.
