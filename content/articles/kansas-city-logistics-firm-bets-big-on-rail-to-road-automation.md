The freight yard on Kansas City's east side hums differently than it did five years ago. Where dispatchers once coordinated truck arrivals with radios and whiteboards, a scheduling system built by Bluestem Freight Systems now predicts container handoffs between rail cars and trucks hours before they happen, shaving what founder Desmond Achebe-Wolfram calls "dead dwell time" out of one of the country's busiest inland freight corridors.

Achebe-Wolfram founded Bluestem Freight Systems in 2016 after a decade working in rail-side logistics operations, frustrated by what he saw as an outdated handoff process between the region's rail lines and its trucking fleets. "Kansas City sits at the crossroads of practically every major freight route in the country, but for years we were wasting that advantage on paperwork and guesswork," he said in an interview at the company's operations center near the Kansas City intermodal facility.

## Solving the Handoff Problem

The company's core product is a coordination platform that gives trucking companies real-time visibility into which rail containers are ready for pickup, replacing a system that once relied on phone calls and manual yard checks. Achebe-Wolfram said the idea came from watching truckers sit idle for hours waiting on containers that hadn't actually cleared for release, burning fuel and driver hours for no reason.

Bluestem Freight Systems now coordinates freight movement for more than 40 trucking companies operating in and around Kansas City, and the platform has expanded to cover yards in St. Louis and a partner facility in Springfield. The company reports that participating carriers have cut average dwell time by nearly a third since adopting the system, a figure that has drawn interest from freight operators well outside Missouri.

## Building a Homegrown Tech Team

Unlike many logistics technology firms that outsource software development, Bluestem Freight Systems built its engineering team locally, recruiting graduates from the University of Missouri-Kansas City and offering relocation packages to draw experienced developers back to the region. Achebe-Wolfram, who grew up in Kansas City's Ivanhoe neighborhood, said that decision was as much personal as strategic.

"I wanted this to be a company where somebody who grew up two miles from here could build a career without moving to Chicago or Dallas," he said. The company now employs 118 people, with roughly a quarter working directly in software and data roles.

## Navigating a Volatile Freight Market

The logistics industry has weathered significant volatility in recent years, with freight rates swinging sharply as shipping demand fluctuates. Achebe-Wolfram said Bluestem Freight Systems has managed those swings by diversifying its client base across agricultural, manufacturing, and retail freight rather than depending heavily on any single sector.

The company is currently piloting a predictive maintenance feature that flags rail cars likely to face mechanical delays before they reach the yard, a capability Achebe-Wolfram believes could become the company's next major product line. He has also begun conversations with regional economic development officials about expanding Bluestem's operations center, a move that could add dozens of jobs to the company's Kansas City workforce over the next two years.

"We're not trying to be the biggest logistics company in the country," Achebe-Wolfram said. "We're trying to be the one that actually understands how freight moves through Kansas City better than anyone else."
