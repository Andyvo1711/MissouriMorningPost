Inside a sleek suite of exam rooms on Forsyth Boulevard in Clayton, Dr. Marissa Odom spends most of her consultations these days talking clients out of doing too much. As the medical director of Forsyth Aesthetic Medicine, she has watched the local med spa market shift dramatically over the past two years, and the shift isn't toward bigger procedures. It's toward smaller ones, done more carefully.

"Five years ago, people came in wanting to look like a different version of themselves," Odom said. "Now they come in with a photo of themselves from a decade ago and say, 'I just want to look like this, but rested.' That's a completely different conversation."

## The Rise of "Skin Cycling" Over Dramatic Overhauls

Odom's practice, which she opened after a decade in emergency medicine, has become one of several med spas clustered in the Clayton business district catering to a client base that skews professional and increasingly younger, with patients now starting preventative treatments like light neuromodulator dosing in their late twenties.

The dominant trend this year, according to Odom and several of her peers in the St. Louis metro area, is what industry publications have taken to calling "skin cycling," a rotating regimen of in-office treatments, like microneedling, low-level laser therapy, and chemical peels, paired with a simplified at-home routine. Clients are moving away from filler-heavy transformations and toward treatments that improve skin texture and tone over months rather than producing an immediate, visible change.

"Filler dissolving has actually become one of our busiest services," Odom said. "We're correcting work that was done five or six years ago, when the trend was to add volume everywhere. Now the trend is balance."

## Training and Oversight Under Scrutiny

The shift has coincided with greater public attention to who is actually performing these procedures. Missouri requires injectable treatments to be performed or directly supervised by a licensed medical provider, and Clayton's more established med spas have leaned into that requirement as a selling point, distinguishing themselves from spas operating with looser oversight.

Nurse injector Colette Vance, who works alongside Odom at Forsyth Aesthetic Medicine, said client education has become a bigger part of her job than the injections themselves. "I probably spend twenty minutes of a thirty-minute appointment just talking through facial anatomy and what's realistic," Vance said. "People watch a lot of content online that isn't necessarily accurate, so part of our role now is just being a trustworthy source of information."

## What's Driving the Caution

Several factors appear to be behind the more conservative approach. Social media scrutiny of overdone cosmetic work has made some clients wary of visible interventions. There's also a generational element: younger clients raised on skincare-focused content tend to prioritize long-term skin health over quick fixes.

Local dermatologist Anthony Buell, who refers patients to several Clayton med spas for adjunct treatments, said he's noticed the same pattern in his own practice. "The clients who come in now are more sophisticated," Buell said. "They're asking about ingredient concentrations, they're asking about downtime, they're asking what actually happens to collagen production over six months. It's a more informed customer than it used to be."

## A More Sustainable Business Model

For Odom, the shift toward moderation hasn't hurt business. If anything, she said, it's built a more loyal client base who return regularly for maintenance treatments rather than making one dramatic purchase and disappearing.

"The goal isn't to make someone look different," Odom said. "It's to help them look like themselves, just with a little more confidence walking into a room. That's a service people keep coming back for."
