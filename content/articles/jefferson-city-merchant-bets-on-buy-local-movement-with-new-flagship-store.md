Elijah Vantongeren opened his first small storefront selling Missouri-made goods and household staples in Jefferson City in 2012, competing against big-box retailers that could undercut his prices on nearly every item he sold. Thirteen years later, his company, Capitol Trade Mercantile, has opened a larger flagship store near the capitol district, a bet that a growing segment of Missouri shoppers will pay a modest premium to support locally owned retail over national chains.

"I can't win on price against a store that buys in container-ship quantities," Vantongeren said, standing among newly stocked shelves in the expanded location. "So I stopped trying to win on price a long time ago."

## Betting on a Buy-Local Shift

Vantongeren's expansion decision came after several years of steady, if modest, growth in his original storefront, during which he noticed a shift in customer conversations toward wanting to know where products came from and who made them. He began stocking more goods directly sourced from Missouri producers, gradually reducing the store's reliance on nationally distributed products that customers could buy anywhere.

"People started asking questions they didn't used to ask, like who actually makes this and where," he said. "That told me there was room to build a bigger store around answering those questions well."

The new flagship location, roughly triple the size of Vantongeren's original store, includes a dedicated section for Missouri-made food products and a rotating display featuring goods from small producers across the state, several of whom Vantongeren discovered through farmers markets and regional trade shows.

## The Risk of Betting Big

The expansion required Vantongeren to take on debt significantly larger than any financing decision he'd made previously, a risk he acknowledges could have sunk the business if customer demand hadn't materialized as projected. He spent nearly a year building relationships with additional Missouri suppliers before signing the lease on the new location, wanting to ensure the store could be stocked convincingly from day one.

"If I'd opened this store half-stocked, waiting to build supplier relationships after the fact, I think it would have failed," Vantongeren said. "You get one chance to make a flagship store look like it belongs."

## Building a Team Around Local Sourcing

Capitol Trade Mercantile now employs 24 people, including a dedicated buyer whose primary job is identifying and vetting new Missouri-based suppliers, a role Vantongeren created specifically to keep the store's local sourcing pipeline growing as the business expands. He said maintaining direct relationships with producers, rather than working through distributors, remains central to the store's identity even as it becomes more logistically complex.

Vantongeren is now considering whether Capitol Trade Mercantile's model could work in a second Missouri city, though he said he has no immediate plans to open an additional location. "I want to make sure this store works exactly the way I want it to first," he said. "Then maybe we talk about a second one."
