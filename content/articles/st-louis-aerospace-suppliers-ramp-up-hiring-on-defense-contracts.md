St. Louis has been a center of American aerospace and defense manufacturing for generations, and the region's supply chain is once again in expansion mode as prime contractors and their subcontractors respond to a fresh round of defense program awards. Smaller machine shops and precision parts manufacturers that feed into larger aerospace assembly operations report order backlogs stretching further into the future than they have in years.

The metro area's aerospace cluster benefits from a dense network of suppliers built up over decades, many of them family-owned businesses that have supported military and commercial aircraft programs across multiple generations of ownership. That institutional knowledge, industry representatives say, has become an important asset as prime contractors look for suppliers who can scale up quickly without sacrificing the quality certifications required for aerospace work.

## A Supplier Base Under Pressure to Scale

Executives at several St. Louis-area aerospace suppliers describe a challenging balancing act: order volumes are strong enough to justify new hiring and, in some cases, equipment investment, but finding workers with the precision manufacturing skills aerospace work demands remains difficult. Certified welders, CNC machinists, and quality inspectors are all in short supply relative to demand across the regional supply chain.

A workforce development official who works with aerospace manufacturers in the region said the industry's hiring needs have pushed several companies to invest directly in training, including partnerships with technical schools and in-house apprenticeship programs. These efforts take time to produce fully qualified workers, creating a lag between order growth and the supplier base's ability to fully capitalize on it.

## Capital Investment Follows Order Books

Beyond hiring, several suppliers have announced plans to add equipment or expand facility square footage to accommodate higher production volumes. Industrial real estate brokers who track the aerospace-adjacent submarket say demand for specialized manufacturing space, particularly buildings with the power capacity and ceiling heights needed for large CNC equipment, has picked up noticeably.

Financing this expansion has generally come through a combination of retained earnings, equipment financing arrangements, and, in some cases, state economic development incentives aimed at retaining defense-related manufacturing jobs in the region. Regional bank lenders who specialize in manufacturing credit describe aerospace supplier loan demand as a bright spot relative to more mixed conditions in other manufacturing subsectors.

## Ripple Effects Across the Regional Economy

The aerospace supply chain's expansion has effects that extend well beyond the companies directly involved in defense contracts. Suppliers of raw materials, specialty coatings, and logistics services that support aerospace manufacturing have also reported increased activity, and local economic development officials point to the sector's high average wages as a meaningful contributor to regional income growth.

Because aerospace manufacturing jobs typically pay well above the regional median wage, officials argue the sector's growth has an outsized positive impact on local tax revenue and consumer spending compared with job growth in lower-wage industries.

## Balancing Optimism With Caution

Industry participants remain mindful that defense spending cycles can shift with changes in federal budget priorities, and few suppliers are betting their entire business on the current wave of contract awards continuing indefinitely. Most describe a strategy of using the current strength to invest in workforce training and equipment modernization that will benefit the business regardless of how defense budgets evolve in coming years, positioning the St. Louis aerospace cluster to remain competitive for the next generation of contract awards.
