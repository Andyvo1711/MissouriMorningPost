Missouri's cattle industry, one of the largest in the nation by head count, is entering a rebuilding phase after several years in which drought conditions pushed many ranchers to sell down their breeding herds rather than pay for costly supplemental feed. With prices for feeder and slaughter cattle holding at historically strong levels, ranchers across the Ozarks and central Missouri are cautiously expanding again.

The state's rolling pastureland and relatively reliable rainfall compared with parts of the Great Plains have long made it attractive for cow-calf operations, which raise calves before selling them into feedlots further west. Missouri consistently ranks among the top states for beef cow numbers, and the current price environment has renewed interest in herd expansion among both established ranchers and younger producers entering the business.

## A Slow and Costly Rebuild

Rebuilding a cattle herd is not something that happens quickly. Ranchers who sold breeding stock during recent dry years now face a multi-year process of retaining heifers, breeding them, and waiting for calves to reach market weight, all while carrying higher costs for replacement animals than they would have paid before the herd liquidation cycle began.

Agricultural lenders who work with Missouri cattle operations describe loan demand as robust but note that many ranchers are being deliberate rather than aggressive about expansion. Interest rates on operating loans remain higher than ranchers grew accustomed to in the previous decade, and feed, fuel, and veterinary costs have all risen, squeezing the margin benefit of strong cattle prices.

## Pasture Conditions and Weather Risk

Grazing conditions across much of Missouri have improved compared with the driest recent years, though ranchers remain wary given how quickly conditions can shift. Extension agents in several counties have encouraged producers to maintain drought contingency plans even during favorable years, pointing to the volatility the region has experienced over the past decade.

Hay prices, which spike sharply during dry spells as ranchers scramble to feed animals without adequate pasture, have moderated somewhat, giving producers more confidence to hold onto female cattle for breeding rather than sending them to market. That decision, multiplied across thousands of Missouri operations, is a key driver of the broader national herd rebuilding trend currently underway.

## Processing and Local Economic Impact

Beyond the ranch gate, Missouri's cattle economy supports a network of sale barns, veterinary practices, feed suppliers, and processing facilities, particularly in the southwestern part of the state. Local economic development officials in cattle-heavy counties say herd expansion tends to have a multiplier effect, supporting everything from equipment dealerships to rural banking branches that depend on agricultural lending.

Smaller regional beef processors have also reported steady demand, benefiting from consumer interest in locally sourced meat even as the broader cattle cycle plays out at a national scale dominated by a handful of large packers.

## Cautious Optimism Heading Forward

Industry groups describe the current mood among Missouri ranchers as cautiously optimistic. Strong prices have provided welcome financial breathing room after difficult years, but elevated costs and the ever-present risk of drought mean few producers are expanding recklessly. Most are taking a measured approach, rebuilding herds gradually while keeping a close eye on both weather patterns and the broader cattle price cycle that history suggests will not stay this favorable indefinitely.
