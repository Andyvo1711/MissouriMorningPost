Most aerospace supply chain startups chase the glamorous end of the industry, pitching investors on drone airframes or composite wing components. Nathaniel Osei-Bramble did the opposite. In 2020, he founded Mizzou Ridge Aerostructures in Columbia to manufacture something far less flashy: certified fasteners and small structural hardware that larger aerospace primes need in enormous volume but rarely want to make themselves.

"Everyone wants to build the wing. Nobody wants to build the bolt that holds the wing together," Osei-Bramble said at the company's facility near the Columbia Regional Airport. "That's exactly why there was room for us."

## Chasing Certification Instead of Contracts

The company's early years were consumed not by sales, but by the painstaking process of securing aerospace quality certifications required to supply parts for commercial and defense aircraft programs. Osei-Bramble, who previously worked as a quality engineer for a supplier in Wichita, said he underestimated how long the certification process would take, requiring nearly two years of documentation, audits, and process validation before Mizzou Ridge Aerostructures could ship its first certified part.

"We had machines running and nothing to sell for the better part of two years," he said. "That's the part of this industry nobody warns you about. You're not really competing on manufacturing skill at first. You're competing on paperwork discipline."

That patience eventually paid off. The company now holds certifications that allow it to supply fastener components to two mid-tier aerospace manufacturers with operations in the central United States, and it recently qualified to bid on subcontracts tied to defense-related aircraft maintenance programs.

## Staying Small on Purpose

Unlike many manufacturing founders eager to scale quickly, Osei-Bramble has deliberately kept Mizzou Ridge Aerostructures's headcount modest, currently around 35 employees, arguing that overextension is what sinks most aerospace suppliers before they establish a reliable customer base. The company has turned down two large orders in the past year because fulfilling them would have required compromising the quality controls its certifications depend on.

"In aerospace, one bad batch of parts can end your certification and your company in the same week," Osei-Bramble said. "I'd rather grow slowly and still be here in ten years."

## Building a Missouri Aerospace Corridor

Osei-Bramble has become an advocate for expanding Missouri's aerospace manufacturing base beyond the St. Louis metro area, arguing that mid-sized cities like Columbia offer lower operating costs and access to skilled labor from the University of Missouri's engineering programs. He has partnered with the university on a materials testing initiative that gives engineering students hands-on experience with aerospace-grade metals.

The company is now exploring a modest facility expansion to add capacity for a new class of titanium fasteners, a product line Osei-Bramble believes could double the company's revenue within three years if certification goes as planned. "We're proving that you don't need to be in Seattle or Wichita to be part of the aerospace supply chain," he said. "You just need to be really, really good at one thing."
