A block off Main Street in historic St. Charles, where cobblestone sidewalks and nineteenth-century storefronts draw a steady stream of weekend visitors, Willow & Stone Day Spa has built a loyal client base without relying on an unusually exotic treatment menu. Instead, owner Sabine Okoro says the spa's differentiator has always been what happens before a client ever reaches the treatment room.

"You can have the best facial technique in Missouri, but if someone feels rushed or ignored at the front desk, that's what they remember," Okoro said. "We train on that as seriously as we train on technique."

## Rethinking the First Five Minutes

Okoro, who spent years managing hospitality operations for a hotel group before opening Willow & Stone eight years ago, brought a hospitality-industry mindset to the spa's front-of-house operations. Every staff member, from receptionists to estheticians, undergoes a service training module focused entirely on guest communication, separate from technical skills training.

"In hotels, we obsessed over the first five minutes of a guest's stay because it sets the tone for everything after," Okoro said. "I applied that same thinking here. The greeting, the transition into the treatment room, the way we explain what's about to happen, all of that shapes whether someone actually relaxes."

Front desk manager Odessa Voss said the spa emphasizes specific, small details that guests often mention in reviews: remembering a returning client's preferred tea, checking in gently if someone seems anxious before a first-time treatment, and never letting a guest wait more than a few minutes without acknowledgment, even during the spa's busiest tourist-season weekends.

## Serving a Mixed Local and Tourist Clientele

Willow & Stone's location in St. Charles' historic district means it serves a dual audience: loyal local clients who visit regularly, and out-of-town visitors exploring the district's shops and restaurants who book a treatment as part of a day trip. Balancing those two groups, Okoro said, requires staff who can adjust their approach depending on whether a guest is a familiar face or a first-timer unfamiliar with spa etiquette.

"A regular client wants efficiency and familiarity. A first-time visitor wants guidance and reassurance," Okoro said. "Our staff have to read that quickly and adjust. That's a skill we actively coach, not something we just hope people pick up naturally."

## Measuring What Matters

The spa collects guest feedback after every visit through a short follow-up survey, and Okoro said she reviews comments personally each week, looking specifically for mentions of staff interactions rather than just treatment outcomes.

"Treatment quality is almost a given at this point. Every spa worth visiting can deliver a good facial or a good massage," Okoro said. "What separates the ones people rave about is whether they felt genuinely cared for during the whole visit, not just the forty-five minutes they were on the table."

Longtime client Rosalind Pruett, who has been visiting Willow & Stone for six years, said the consistency is what keeps her coming back over spas closer to her home in St. Peters. "It's a bit of a drive for me, but I've never once felt like just another appointment there," Pruett said. "They remember things about you. That matters more than people realize."

## Expanding the Training Model

Okoro said she's now developing a formal customer service curriculum she hopes to eventually offer to other independent spas in the St. Charles area, believing the hospitality-focused approach is a differentiator smaller spas can adopt without significant added cost.

"You don't need a bigger treatment menu to compete," Okoro said. "You need people who genuinely make guests feel taken care of. That's replicable, and it's underrated."
