Saint Louis University has opened the doors of a new health sciences building on its midtown St. Louis campus, adding much-needed classroom and laboratory space for programs that university officials say have outgrown their previous facilities over the past decade.

The building houses simulation labs designed to mimic hospital and clinical environments, along with classrooms configured for small-group, case-based learning that has become increasingly common in health professions education. Programs moving into the new space include nursing, physical therapy, and physician assistant studies, all of which have seen rising application numbers in recent years.

## Addressing Space Constraints

University administrators say the project was driven largely by necessity. Health sciences enrollment across the country has grown steadily as students respond to persistent workforce shortages in nursing and allied health fields, and Saint Louis University's existing facilities had become a bottleneck for expanding class sizes.

"We were turning away qualified applicants in some programs simply because we didn't have the lab capacity to accommodate them," said a spokesperson for the university's health sciences division. "This building lets us open up more seats without compromising the hands-on training that these programs depend on."

The simulation labs are outfitted with programmable manikins that can mimic a range of patient conditions, from cardiac emergencies to complications during childbirth, giving students the chance to practice clinical decision-making in a controlled setting before working with actual patients during clinical rotations. Faculty say the technology also allows instructors to record and review student performance, a feature that has become a standard part of health professions curricula at many universities.

## A Boost for Regional Health Workforce Needs

Hospital systems across the St. Louis region have expressed interest in the expansion, given ongoing efforts to recruit and retain nursing staff amid national workforce shortages. University officials say they have fielded conversations with several regional health systems about expanding clinical placement partnerships to accommodate the larger cohorts the new building will support.

Students who toured the facility before its official opening said the upgrade was long overdue. "The old labs were fine, but you'd have three or four groups rotating through the same small room," said one nursing student in her junior year. "Having dedicated space for our program means we're not constantly working around each other's schedules."

The university has not disclosed the total cost of the project but described it as one of the largest facilities investments in the health sciences division in recent years, funded through a combination of donor contributions and university capital funds. Construction took a little over two years, with some delays attributed to supply chain issues affecting specialized medical simulation equipment.

Looking ahead, university officials say they expect the new building to support gradual enrollment growth across health sciences programs over the next several admissions cycles, though they caution that any expansion will remain tied to maintaining accreditation standards and adequate clinical placement capacity in the region's hospitals and clinics. Additional signage and wayfinding updates around the new building are expected to be completed before the fall semester begins.
