A radiologist reviewing a chest scan now often sees more than the raw image on the screen. In a growing number of Missouri hospitals, that scan arrives with an overlay generated by artificial intelligence software, flagging areas that warrant closer attention before a human physician has even begun a formal review. Several Missouri health systems have begun piloting these tools over the past year, part of a broader national trend toward AI-assisted diagnostics.

The technology varies by hospital and use case, but generally functions as a triage layer rather than a diagnostic authority on its own. Software trained on large sets of medical imaging can flag scans showing patterns associated with conditions such as pulmonary nodules, certain fractures, or signs of internal bleeding, prioritizing those cases for faster physician review among a queue that might otherwise be handled strictly in the order received.

## Assisting, Not Replacing, Physicians

Hospital officials are careful to frame the technology as a support tool rather than a replacement for clinical judgment, emphasizing that every AI-flagged result still requires review and confirmation by a licensed physician before it informs a treatment decision. "The software doesn't make a diagnosis, it makes a suggestion about where to look first," said a radiology department chief at a Missouri hospital piloting the technology. "The physician still has to look at the full picture, including the patient's history and symptoms, before anything becomes a diagnosis."

Emergency departments have found particular value in the faster triage capability, since identifying a critical finding, such as signs of a stroke or a collapsed lung, minutes earlier can meaningfully change a patient's treatment timeline. Several hospitals report that AI-assisted flagging has helped reduce the time between a scan being taken and a critical finding reaching the treating physician, though officials caution that results vary by hospital and case type.

## Addressing Workforce Pressure

Part of the appeal for Missouri hospitals lies in ongoing workforce shortages among radiologists and other specialists, a challenge particularly acute in rural parts of the state where hospitals may have limited or no on-site imaging specialists. Some rural facilities have begun using AI-assisted preliminary review to help support remote radiologists managing a high volume of scans from multiple hospitals, potentially improving turnaround times for patients who would otherwise wait longer for a formal reading.

"We don't have the luxury of a radiologist sitting in-house around the clock," said a rural hospital administrator exploring similar technology. "Anything that helps our on-call radiologist prioritize the most urgent cases first is valuable, especially overnight when volumes can back up."

## Questions About Oversight and Accuracy

Adoption has not been without caution. Hospital ethics committees and medical staff leadership in several systems have emphasized the need for ongoing accuracy monitoring, noting that AI tools can produce false positives or, more concerning, miss findings outside the patterns they were trained to recognize. Physicians involved in the pilots stress that the tools are being layered onto, not substituted for, standard review protocols, and that any expansion of AI use will depend on continued validation against physician-confirmed outcomes.

Hospital leaders describe the current rollout as an early but important step, with most systems planning to expand AI-assisted tools gradually into additional departments only after demonstrating consistent, measurable benefit in the areas where they have already been deployed.
