When Marguerite Delacroix-Hoyt left a corporate agronomy job in 2019 to start selling soil moisture sensors out of a converted garage in St. Louis's Dutchtown neighborhood, most of her former colleagues assumed the venture would fold within a year. Seven years later, her company, Bootheel Harvest Analytics, supplies real-time field data to more than 900 farms stretching from the Missouri Bootheel to the corn belt counties north of the Missouri River.

"Farmers don't need more data. They need the right three numbers at the right moment," Delacroix-Hoyt said from the company's headquarters near the Cortex Innovation District, where a wall-mounted dashboard tracks soil moisture and nitrogen readings streaming in from thousands of field sensors. That philosophy of ruthless simplicity, she said, is what separated her company from better-funded agtech startups that tried to sell farmers on complexity instead of clarity.

## From Spreadsheet to Statewide Network

Bootheel Harvest Analytics began as a side project while Delacroix-Hoyt was still consulting for row-crop operations in southeast Missouri. She noticed that the same yield-limiting problems kept appearing on farms that had no way of detecting them until harvest, when it was too late to act. Her first product was a single sensor paired with a text-message alert system, built with a University of Missouri engineering graduate she hired as the company's first employee.

The company's growth accelerated in 2023 when it secured a distribution partnership with a regional co-op network, putting its hardware in the hands of farmers who had previously relied entirely on visual field scouting. Revenue has grown roughly 40 percent year over year since, and the company now employs 62 people, about a third of them in field service roles that keep sensor networks running through Missouri's humid summers and freezing winters.

## Betting on Missouri Over Silicon Valley

Delacroix-Hoyt has turned down at least two acquisition offers from larger agriculture technology firms based in California, arguing that a St. Louis headquarters gives the company an advantage that outside owners would eventually erode. "Our engineers can be in a soybean field in Bollinger County by lunchtime and back in the office by five," she said. "Try doing that from Palo Alto."

That local focus has shaped hiring as well. The company recruits heavily from Missouri's agricultural and engineering programs, including Missouri State University and Southeast Missouri State University, and runs a paid internship program specifically for students from rural counties who might otherwise leave the state for tech jobs.

Industry analysts who track agricultural technology investment say Bootheel Harvest Analytics has succeeded in part by resisting pressure to expand into unrelated product lines. The company still sells only soil and moisture sensing hardware paired with its proprietary analytics platform, a narrow focus Delacroix-Hoyt defends as central to her business philosophy.

## Looking Toward Expansion

The company is now piloting a program with grain elevators in the Bootheel to link field-level sensor data directly to storage and pricing decisions, a move Delacroix-Hoyt believes could open a second major revenue stream. She has also begun exploring partnerships with irrigation equipment manufacturers to build sensor compatibility directly into new machinery sold in the state.

Despite the company's growth, Delacroix-Hoyt said she has no plans to relocate its headquarters or list the company publicly. "We built this to serve Missouri ground first," she said. "Everything else is secondary."
