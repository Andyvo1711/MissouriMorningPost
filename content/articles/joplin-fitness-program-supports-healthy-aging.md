Inside a converted retail space near downtown Joplin, a class of a dozen participants, most in their sixties, seventies, and eighties, moves through a slow, deliberate sequence of balance exercises, some holding onto sturdy chairs for support, others working freestyle in the center of the room. This is Evergreen Movement Studio's signature "Steady & Strong" class, and on any given weekday morning, it's one of the studio's most consistently full sessions.

Founder Lena Marchetti, a physical therapist who spent over a decade working in outpatient rehabilitation before opening Evergreen three years ago, said she built the studio specifically to address what she saw as a gap in fitness options for older adults in the Joplin area.

## Shifting the Goal Away From Weight Loss

"So much of fitness marketing is built around weight loss or aesthetics," Marchetti said. "For a lot of older adults, that's just not the relevant goal. What matters is whether you can get up from the floor unassisted, whether you can carry your own groceries, whether your balance is good enough that a stumble doesn't turn into a hospital visit."

Evergreen's classes are built around functional movement patterns rather than traditional strength training metrics, incorporating balance work, resistance band exercises, and mobility drills designed to mirror the physical demands of daily life, from rising from a chair to reaching an overhead shelf.

The studio also offers a falls-prevention assessment for new clients, screening for balance deficits and mobility limitations before recommending a class track, a service Marchetti said has become increasingly requested as local physicians and physical therapists have begun referring patients directly to Evergreen.

## A Community as Much as a Studio

Beyond the physical benefits, instructor Odalys Whitfield said the studio has become an important social outlet for many of its regular attendees, some of whom live alone and describe the twice-weekly classes as a highlight of their week.

"We've had clients tell us this is the only time all week they have real, sustained conversation with other people," Whitfield said. "That's not a small thing. Isolation is its own health risk for older adults, and we didn't fully anticipate how much that social piece would matter when we designed the program."

The studio has leaned into that community element deliberately, hosting a monthly potluck after Friday classes and organizing an annual walking group that participates in a local charity fundraiser each fall.

## Addressing Skepticism From a Cautious Demographic

Marchetti acknowledged that convincing older adults to try a fitness studio for the first time, particularly those with no prior exercise habit, required patience and a different marketing approach than a typical gym would use.

"A lot of our clients tell us they were nervous about looking foolish or getting hurt," Marchetti said. "We spend a lot of time in that first session just building trust, showing people that every exercise has a modification, and that nobody in the room is judging anyone else's pace."

Client Herbert Doss, seventy-four, said he joined Evergreen after a fall at home led his doctor to recommend a structured balance program. "I was skeptical at first," Doss said. "But I've noticed a real difference in my confidence just walking around my own house. That's worth more to me at this point than looking a certain way."

## Looking Ahead

Marchetti said Evergreen plans to add a second falls-prevention specialist to its staff this year and is exploring a partnership with a local senior center to bring modified versions of its classes to residents with more limited mobility.

"Aging well isn't about fighting the process," Marchetti said. "It's about staying strong enough to keep doing the things that make your life yours."
