Colette Fairweather-Nnamdi sold her first bottles of pepper sauce from a folding table at the Jefferson City farmers market in 2015, mixing batches in a rented commercial kitchen after her day job. Eleven years later, her company, Capitol Creek Provisions, runs its own bottling line and supplies condiments and marinades to grocery chains across Missouri, with distribution expanding into Kansas and Illinois.

"I never set out to build a factory," Fairweather-Nnamdi said, watching bottles move down the company's newly installed filling line. "I just kept saying yes to the next order, and eventually the next order required a building."

## From Farmers Market to Food Safety Certification

The company's growth forced Fairweather-Nnamdi to navigate a regulatory landscape she hadn't anticipated when she started. Scaling beyond farmers market sales required securing food safety certifications, passing facility inspections, and reformulating recipes to meet shelf-stability standards for retail distribution, a process she said took nearly eighteen months and drained most of the company's early savings.

"There's a version of this business where I stayed small forever and never had to deal with any of that," she said. "But I wanted people in Springfield and Cape Girardeau to be able to buy what I was making, not just people who happened to be at the market on a Saturday morning."

Capitol Creek Provisions now produces twelve product lines, from barbecue sauces to pepper-based hot sauces, all manufactured at its Jefferson City facility using produce sourced primarily from Missouri farms. Fairweather-Nnamdi said maintaining that local sourcing relationship, even as the company scaled, has become a key part of its brand identity with retailers.

## Competing for Shelf Space

Breaking into grocery store shelf space proved harder than building the product itself, according to Fairweather-Nnamdi, who spent nearly two years cold-calling regional grocery buyers before securing her first major retail placement. The turning point came when a Columbia-based grocery chain agreed to a trial placement in ten stores, an order that sold out within weeks and led to a statewide distribution agreement.

"Buyers don't care how good your sauce tastes if they don't think you can deliver five hundred cases on time, every time," she said. "Proving reliability mattered more than proving flavor, which surprised me."

## Building a Team Rooted in Jefferson City

The company now employs 28 people at its Jefferson City facility, most of them hired locally, and Fairweather-Nnamdi has prioritized keeping production in the capital city even as larger contract manufacturers elsewhere offered to produce her products at lower cost. "Moving production out of Jefferson City would save money, but it would also break the story I'm selling," she said. "People buy this because it's actually made here."

Fairweather-Nnamdi is currently negotiating with a distributor to expand Capitol Creek Provisions's reach into Arkansas and Oklahoma, a step she describes as the natural next test of whether the company can maintain its quality standards while operating at a larger scale. "Every stage of this business has felt like starting over," she said. "I assume this next one will too."
