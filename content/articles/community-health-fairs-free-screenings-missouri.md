A line formed early outside a church fellowship hall this month as residents waited for their turn at a folding table set up with a blood pressure cuff and a clipboard of intake forms. The scene has repeated itself across Missouri this summer, as hospitals, county health departments, and community organizations host a wave of free health fairs aimed at reaching residents who may otherwise go months, or years, without a basic health screening.

Organizers say the events serve a specific gap in the healthcare system: residents who lack a regular primary care provider, who are uninsured or underinsured, or who simply have not prioritized preventive checkups amid the demands of daily life. By offering blood pressure checks, blood sugar testing, cholesterol screening, and basic health education in familiar community settings, organizers hope to catch warning signs before they become medical emergencies.

## Meeting People Where They Already Are

Health fair organizers have increasingly focused on locations residents already trust and frequent, including churches, community centers, and local grocery store parking lots, rather than asking people to travel to an unfamiliar clinical setting. Volunteers say this approach has proven effective at reaching people who might feel intimidated or distrustful of a traditional healthcare environment.

"A lot of people who come through our screening line haven't seen a doctor in years, not because they don't care about their health, but because of cost, transportation, or just not knowing where to start," said a community health nurse who has organized several fairs this summer. "When we set up somewhere familiar, somewhere they already trust, that barrier comes down."

## Catching Problems Early

Volunteers report that a meaningful share of attendees at each event are found to have elevated blood pressure or blood sugar readings that had gone previously undetected. Those individuals are typically given information on local low-cost or sliding-scale clinics and encouraged to follow up for a full diagnostic workup, since a single screening reading cannot confirm a diagnosis on its own.

"We're not diagnosing anyone at a folding table in a parking lot," said a nurse practitioner who volunteers regularly at these events. "But we can absolutely flag a number that's concerning and connect that person with somewhere to go next. For some people, that's the first step they've taken toward addressing a health issue in years."

## A Collaborative Effort

The health fairs typically bring together a mix of partners, including hospital systems, local health departments, pharmacy students, and nursing students seeking hands-on community health experience. Organizers say the collaborative model helps stretch limited resources further, allowing events to offer a broader range of screenings than any single organization could provide alone.

Attendance at several fairs this summer has exceeded organizer expectations, prompting some organizations to consider adding additional dates before the year is out. County health officials note that while a single afternoon of screening cannot replace consistent primary care, the events can serve as an important entry point for residents who have otherwise fallen outside the healthcare system, offering both a first health check and a clearer path toward ongoing care.
