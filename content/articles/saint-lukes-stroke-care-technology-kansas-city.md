In stroke care, physicians often repeat a simple phrase to describe the stakes: time is brain. Every minute a stroke goes untreated, more brain tissue can be lost. Saint Luke's Health System in Kansas City has spent the past several months installing new imaging and analysis technology aimed at cutting the time between a patient's arrival and the start of treatment, part of a broader push to strengthen its comprehensive stroke center capabilities.

The upgraded system uses advanced imaging software to rapidly process brain scans and flag signs of blocked or damaged blood vessels, generating results that clinicians say can be reviewed in a fraction of the time older systems required. For patients who may be candidates for clot-dissolving medication or mechanical clot retrieval procedures, that faster turnaround can directly affect eligibility, since many stroke treatments are only effective within narrow windows after symptom onset.

## A Regional Hub for Stroke Transfers

Saint Luke's has long served as a receiving hospital for stroke patients transferred from smaller hospitals across western Missouri and eastern Kansas, many of which lack the specialized neurology staff or equipment needed to manage complex cases locally. The new imaging technology is integrated with a telestroke network that allows emergency physicians at partner hospitals to consult with Saint Luke's neurologists remotely, sharing scan results instantly rather than waiting for images to be manually transferred.

"When a rural ER physician is looking at a patient who might be having a stroke, every extra phone call or delayed image transfer works against that patient," said a neurologist involved in the stroke program's technology upgrade. "Being able to pull up a scan on a tablet within minutes of it being taken changes the entire pace of decision-making."

## Training Staff Across the System

Rolling out new technology is only part of the effort. Saint Luke's has also run system-wide training sessions for emergency department staff, stroke coordinators, and radiology technicians to ensure the new tools are used consistently across its network of hospitals. Stroke coordinators say standardizing how scans are ordered and reviewed has been just as important as the software itself, since inconsistent protocols can erase much of the time savings the technology is meant to provide.

A stroke program coordinator described the training process as intensive but necessary. "The tool is only as good as the workflow around it," she said. "We spent as much time on process mapping, who calls whom, who reviews what first, as we did on the technology rollout itself."

## Community Impact

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of long-term disability in Missouri, and health officials note that outcomes are often worse in rural areas where access to specialized stroke care has historically lagged behind urban centers. By strengthening the connection between Kansas City-based specialists and outlying hospitals, Saint Luke's officials say they hope to narrow that gap, even for patients who never physically travel to the flagship campus.

Community health advocates have welcomed the investment, though some note that public awareness of stroke symptoms remains just as critical as hospital technology. Recognizing early warning signs, such as sudden facial drooping, arm weakness, or slurred speech, and calling for emergency transport quickly, still determines whether a patient reaches a stroke center in time to benefit from the very tools now being installed. Saint Luke's has paired the technology rollout with renewed public education campaigns across the Kansas City metro area, aiming to ensure the faster treatment window translates into faster patient action as well.
