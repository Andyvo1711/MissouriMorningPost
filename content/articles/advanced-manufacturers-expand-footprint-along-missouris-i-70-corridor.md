The stretch of Interstate 70 connecting St. Louis and Kansas City has become one of Missouri's most consistently active corridors for manufacturing investment, with companies producing everything from precision components to industrial equipment expanding operations in towns along the route. Economic development officials describe the corridor as benefiting from a combination of central geography, available industrial land, and a manufacturing workforce with deep roots in the region.

Unlike the flashier headlines generated by single mega-projects elsewhere in the country, much of Missouri's manufacturing growth along I-70 has come through incremental expansions: existing plants adding production lines, mid-sized suppliers building new facilities, and smaller companies relocating from more expensive coastal markets in search of lower operating costs.

## Suppliers Feeding Larger Supply Chains

Many of the manufacturers expanding along the corridor operate as suppliers to larger original equipment manufacturers in industries like automotive, agricultural machinery, and industrial equipment. That supplier relationship gives Missouri's manufacturing base a degree of insulation from any single industry's downturn, since plants in the state often serve multiple end markets simultaneously.

A manufacturing consultant who works with Missouri firms on site selection and expansion planning said the state's central location has become more valuable as companies rethink supply chain resilience following disruptions in recent years. Being within a day's drive of a large share of the U.S. population, the consultant noted, has become a selling point that increasingly outweighs modest differences in tax incentives between competing states.

## Workforce Remains the Central Challenge

Despite the investment momentum, manufacturers along the corridor consistently cite workforce availability as their top operational concern. Skilled trades positions, including machinists, welders, and industrial maintenance technicians, remain difficult to fill even as wages for these roles have climbed. Several counties along the corridor have partnered with community colleges to expand apprenticeship and technical training programs aimed at building a homegrown pipeline of manufacturing workers.

Employers say these partnerships take years to bear fruit, but early results suggest they are helping stabilize hiring in markets that had previously relied heavily on workers commuting from larger metro areas. Retention has also improved at plants that have invested in modernized equipment and cleaner working environments, which manufacturing recruiters say makes a measurable difference in attracting younger workers to production roles.

## Capital Investment and Local Financing

Much of the expansion activity has been financed through a mix of company capital, state and local incentive packages, and financing from regional banks that specialize in industrial and equipment lending. Community bankers active in the corridor say loan demand from manufacturing clients has remained solid, though some smaller manufacturers have grown more cautious about large capital equipment purchases given higher borrowing costs.

Local governments along the corridor have also competed for projects using a mix of property tax abatements and infrastructure investment, particularly for water, sewer, and rail spur improvements needed to support larger industrial users.

## A Steady, if Unglamorous, Growth Story

Economic development officials describe Missouri's I-70 manufacturing growth as a steady accumulation of smaller wins rather than a single transformative project. That incremental pattern, they argue, may ultimately prove more durable than a strategy built around chasing one large facility, since the corridor's diversified manufacturing base is less exposed to the risk of a single company's decision to relocate or downsize. For now, the towns along I-70 continue adding manufacturing jobs and industrial square footage at a pace that has made the corridor one of the more closely watched economic development stories in the state.
