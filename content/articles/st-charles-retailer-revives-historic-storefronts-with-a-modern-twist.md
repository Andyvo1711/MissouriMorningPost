When Harold Bettencourt III bought his first storefront on St. Charles's historic South Main Street in 2014, the 1870s brick building had been vacant for six years and required a nearly complete structural overhaul before it could open as anything at all. Twelve years later, his company, Main Street Provisioners Co., operates four storefronts along the same historic corridor, selling everything from Missouri-made goods to home furnishings, and has become a model other small retail groups in the region study closely.

"Everybody told me historic buildings were more trouble than they were worth," Bettencourt said, standing in the doorway of his original store, its restored tin ceiling visible above modern retail fixtures. "They weren't wrong about the trouble. They were wrong about the worth."

## Balancing Preservation With Practicality

Renovating historic storefronts required Bettencourt to navigate a maze of preservation guidelines, structural repairs, and financing challenges that conventional retail lenders were often reluctant to underwrite. He eventually secured historic tax credit financing for his first project, a process he said took nearly a year longer than a standard commercial loan but ultimately made the numbers work.

"Historic preservation financing is not for people in a hurry," Bettencourt said. "But it's often the only financing that actually makes these buildings affordable to restore."

Each subsequent storefront acquisition became somewhat easier as Bettencourt built relationships with local preservation officials and developed a reputation for completing restorations that met both historic guidelines and modern retail functionality. Main Street Provisioners Co. now generates enough foot traffic that Bettencourt has been approached by several national retail chains interested in the corridor, offers he has consistently declined.

## Curating Over Chasing Volume

Bettencourt has deliberately avoided turning any of his four storefronts over to national brands, instead curating a mix of Missouri-made products, locally sourced goods, and a small selection of imported items that fit the shops' overall aesthetic. He argues this curation, rather than simply maximizing square footage revenue, is what keeps customers returning and differentiates South Main Street from more generic shopping districts.

"A national chain would probably pay more rent than what I'm making from my own inventory in some of these spaces," he said. "But then it stops being South Main Street and starts being any strip mall in the country."

## Anchoring a Broader Revitalization

Bettencourt's storefronts now employ 31 people across the four locations, and he has become an informal advisor to other property owners along the corridor considering similar restoration projects. The city's tourism office has credited Main Street Provisioners Co.'s renovations with contributing to a broader increase in foot traffic along South Main Street over the past several years.

Bettencourt is currently evaluating a fifth storefront acquisition, a building that has sat vacant for over a decade, though he acknowledges the project would require an even more extensive restoration than his previous efforts. "Every building I buy, I think this might be the one that finally breaks me," he said. "So far, none of them have."
