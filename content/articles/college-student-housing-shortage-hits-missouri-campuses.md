A growing number of Missouri universities are facing on-campus housing shortages, as enrollment growth at several institutions has outpaced the number of available dormitory beds, prompting some schools to lease off-campus apartment units or convert underused spaces into temporary student housing.

The squeeze reflects a broader national pattern in higher education, where enrollment increases at many public and private universities have collided with residence halls that, in some cases, were built decades ago and never significantly expanded. Housing officials at several Missouri campuses say they have had to get creative in recent years to guarantee housing for incoming freshmen, a commitment many universities make as part of their recruitment promises to prospective students and families.

## Universities Turn to Off-Campus Solutions

At more than one Missouri campus, housing administrators say they have signed leasing agreements with nearby apartment complexes to house overflow students, particularly upperclassmen who are less likely to be guaranteed a traditional dormitory spot. While these arrangements help address immediate capacity issues, officials acknowledge they come with logistical complications, including transportation to campus and ensuring off-campus students still have access to the same support services available to those living in traditional residence halls.

"We never want a student to show up in August without a place to live," said a housing director at one Missouri public university. "But leasing off-campus units is really a stopgap. It's not a substitute for building the capacity we actually need long-term."

Some universities have also converted spaces originally intended for other purposes, including former guest suites and underused lounge areas, into additional student housing, a temporary measure that administrators say is not a sustainable long-term solution but has helped bridge the gap while longer-term construction plans are developed.

## Construction Plans Move Slowly

Several Missouri universities have announced plans for new residence hall construction in response to the shortage, though university officials caution that these projects typically take years to move from planning through completion, meaning near-term relief is limited. Financing new dormitory construction has also proven challenging for some public universities, which must weigh capital spending on housing against other pressing infrastructure needs, including aging classroom buildings and laboratory facilities.

Students affected by the shortage describe a range of experiences. "I ended up in a triple room that was originally built for two people, which was a rough way to start freshman year," said one sophomore at a Missouri public university. "Some of my friends who lived off campus in leased apartments actually seemed to have it a bit easier, even with the extra commute."

Housing officials say they are closely monitoring enrollment projections to better anticipate future housing needs, noting that overcorrecting and building excess capacity carries its own financial risks if enrollment growth slows in future years. In the meantime, several universities say they are prioritizing guaranteed housing for first-year and transfer students, while encouraging upperclassmen to consider off-campus options earlier in the housing search process to help ease pressure on limited on-campus inventory.
