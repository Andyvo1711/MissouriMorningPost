Downtown St. Louis's commercial real estate market has struggled with elevated office vacancy rates in recent years, a challenge shared by many mid-sized American cities as remote and hybrid work arrangements have become more permanent features of the corporate landscape. In response, a growing number of developers and city officials are turning to office-to-residential conversion as a strategy to fill aging buildings and add housing stock downtown.

The concept is straightforward in theory: take underused office towers, many built decades ago with floor plates and infrastructure poorly suited to modern office tenants, and reconfigure them into apartments. In practice, conversion projects are technically complex and expensive, requiring extensive rework of plumbing, electrical systems, and floor layouts to accommodate residential use.

## Which Buildings Make Sense to Convert

Not every office building is a good conversion candidate. Real estate developers say older buildings with smaller floor plates and more windows per square foot tend to convert more successfully than newer glass towers with large, deep floor plans that are difficult to bring natural light into individual apartment units. St. Louis's downtown, with its mix of early twentieth-century buildings alongside more modern towers, has a meaningful inventory of structures that fit the more favorable profile.

A commercial real estate developer active in several downtown conversion projects said the economics typically only work with a combination of favorable acquisition pricing, since distressed office assets can often be purchased well below replacement cost, and public incentives that help offset the higher construction costs conversions carry compared with ground-up residential development.

## City Incentives and Policy Support

St. Louis city officials have pursued various incentive tools to encourage conversion activity, including tax abatement programs aimed specifically at adaptive reuse projects downtown. Proponents argue that converting vacant office space into housing serves multiple public policy goals simultaneously: reducing blight from empty buildings, adding downtown housing stock, and supporting the retail and restaurant businesses that benefit from a larger residential population living within walking distance.

Critics of conversion incentives caution that public subsidies need to be carefully structured to ensure they are actually necessary to make projects feasible, rather than simply padding developer returns on projects that might have proceeded anyway. City finance officials say they have tried to calibrate incentive packages project by project based on detailed underwriting of conversion costs.

## Financing Remains a Hurdle

Beyond incentives, conversion projects still require significant construction financing, and lenders active in the space describe conversion loans as requiring more scrutiny than typical multifamily construction financing given the added complexity and cost uncertainty involved in retrofitting older buildings. Several regional and national lenders have nonetheless shown willingness to finance well-structured conversion projects in downtown St. Louis, viewing the combination of discounted acquisition costs and growing rental demand for downtown apartments as an attractive risk-adjusted opportunity.

## A Long-Term Strategy for a Changing Downtown

Even optimistic proponents of office-to-residential conversion acknowledge it will not single-handedly solve downtown St. Louis's office vacancy challenges. Only a subset of the market's vacant office inventory is realistically convertible, and projects take years to plan, finance, and execute. Still, city officials and developers alike view the strategy as an important tool for reshaping downtown's economic base over the coming decade, gradually shifting parts of the district from a purely commercial daytime economy toward a more mixed residential and commercial neighborhood that can support activity around the clock.
