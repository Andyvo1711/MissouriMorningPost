Lincoln University in Jefferson City, one of Missouri's two historically Black colleges and universities, has expanded a scholarship fund aimed at supporting first-generation college students, part of a broader institutional effort to reduce the financial barriers that often contribute to students leaving before completing a degree.

University officials say the expanded fund draws on a combination of alumni donations, state support, and new private contributions secured over the past year. The scholarships are targeted specifically at students who are the first in their families to attend college, a group that research has consistently shown faces higher dropout risk due to factors ranging from financial strain to limited access to informal guidance about navigating college bureaucracy.

## Addressing Financial and Institutional Barriers

"A lot of the students we serve aren't just dealing with tuition costs. They're dealing with the fact that nobody in their family has been through this process before," said a Lincoln University financial aid administrator. "Scholarship dollars help, but we've also worked to pair funding with mentorship, because covering costs alone doesn't solve every barrier a first-generation student runs into."

University data shared by officials indicates that first-generation students who receive both financial support and structured mentoring through the program have persisted toward graduation at notably higher rates than those who receive scholarship funding alone, a finding that has shaped how the university structures the expanded initiative going forward. Students accepted into the scholarship program are now paired with faculty or staff mentors for their first two years on campus.

Lincoln University, founded in 1866 by Black veterans of the U.S. Colored Troops, has a long history of serving students who might not otherwise have access to higher education, a mission university leaders say remains central to its identity even as the institution has grown and diversified its academic offerings over the decades.

## Broader Efforts Around Retention and Completion

The scholarship expansion is part of a wider set of retention initiatives at Lincoln, including expanded tutoring services and a redesigned first-year experience course intended to help new students build academic and time-management skills before they encounter the toughest parts of their coursework. University officials say these efforts are informed by a broader national conversation among HBCUs about improving graduation rates without compromising access for students who might not meet traditional admissions benchmarks at more selective institutions.

Students who have benefited from the scholarship fund describe it as transformative. "Without this, I honestly don't know if I would have been able to stay enrolled full-time," said one junior studying elementary education, who works part-time in addition to her coursework. "Having a mentor who checks in with me regularly has mattered almost as much as the money itself."

University leaders say they hope to continue growing the fund through additional fundraising campaigns aimed at alumni and regional employers, with a long-term goal of reducing the share of students who leave the university due to financial hardship. Officials caution that scholarship expansion alone cannot fully close persistent completion gaps, but describe it as a meaningful and necessary piece of a larger, multi-year strategy.
