Freeman Health System has finished a long-planned expansion of its emergency department in Joplin, adding treatment space that hospital leaders say was badly needed after years of rising patient volumes strained the facility's original footprint. The project adds new treatment bays along with a separate fast-track area intended for patients with less severe conditions who do not require full trauma-level resources.

Freeman serves as a major hospital hub for southwest Missouri, drawing patients not only from Joplin but from surrounding rural counties in Missouri, Kansas, and Oklahoma where emergency care options are limited. Hospital administrators say emergency department visits have climbed steadily over the past several years, driven in part by population growth in the region and by patients from smaller communities whose local emergency rooms have closed or reduced services.

## Separating Urgent From Emergent

The new fast-track area is designed to address a common bottleneck in emergency departments nationwide: patients with minor injuries or illnesses, such as simple fractures, minor lacerations, or mild respiratory infections, often wait behind more critical cases, sometimes for hours. By creating a dedicated space and staffing model for these lower-acuity visits, Freeman officials say they expect to reduce overall wait times across the department.

"When someone comes in with chest pain, that patient needs to be seen immediately, and rightly so," said an emergency department director involved in planning the expansion. "But that shouldn't mean someone with a sprained ankle sits in a waiting room for four hours. Separating those patient flows lets us treat everyone more efficiently."

## A Regional Trauma Resource

Freeman's emergency department also functions as a verified trauma center, handling serious injuries transferred from smaller hospitals throughout the Four States region, an area where Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, and Arkansas meet. The expansion includes additional trauma bays equipped with updated resuscitation equipment, part of an effort to maintain the hospital's trauma designation amid growing patient volumes.

Paramedics who regularly transport patients to Freeman say the added capacity should ease a persistent challenge: ambulance diversion, when an emergency department becomes so full that incoming ambulances must be redirected to a different hospital, sometimes adding significant time to a patient's path to care. "Diversion isn't just an inconvenience, it can be the difference in a critical case," said a regional EMS coordinator. "More capacity at Freeman means fewer situations where we're forced to look elsewhere."

## Staffing to Match the Space

Hospital officials acknowledge that physical expansion alone does not solve emergency department strain, and Freeman has paired the construction project with an ongoing effort to recruit additional emergency physicians, nurses, and technicians. Like many Missouri hospitals, Freeman has faced competition for emergency medicine staff amid broader healthcare workforce shortages, and administrators say attracting talent to southwest Missouri has required emphasizing both the region's lower cost of living and the hospital's expanded resources.

Community leaders in Joplin have welcomed the expansion, noting that the emergency department serves as a safety net not just for the city but for a wide swath of rural southwest Missouri. Freeman officials say they will continue monitoring patient volumes to determine whether further expansion will be necessary in the years ahead.
