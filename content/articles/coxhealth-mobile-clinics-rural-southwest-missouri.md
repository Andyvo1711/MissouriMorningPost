On a recent weekday, a converted medical vehicle parked outside a community center in a small town outside Springfield, and within an hour a line had formed of residents waiting for blood pressure checks, diabetes screenings, and basic primary care consultations. The visit was part of a new mobile clinic program CoxHealth has launched to reach communities across southwest Missouri where the nearest full-time physician's office can be a thirty-minute drive or more.

CoxHealth, based in Springfield, has served as a regional hub for much of southwest Missouri for decades, but hospital officials say geography remains one of the biggest obstacles to consistent preventive care in the surrounding counties. The mobile clinic program is designed to close that gap by bringing a rotating schedule of visits to towns too small to support a standalone clinic of their own.

## Filling Gaps Between Visits

Each mobile clinic stop is staffed by a nurse practitioner and a medical assistant, equipped to handle routine checkups, chronic disease management, and basic diagnostic testing. Patients needing more advanced care, such as imaging or specialist consultation, are referred to CoxHealth's main campus or connected via telehealth from the mobile unit itself, combining in-person and virtual care in a single visit.

"A lot of what we're catching out here isn't dramatic, it's the routine stuff that gets missed when someone doesn't have an easy way to see a doctor regularly," said a nurse practitioner who staffs the mobile unit on its rotating schedule. "Untreated high blood pressure, blood sugar that's been creeping up for months, these are things we can address early if people can actually get seen."

## Building Trust in Small Communities

Hospital officials say establishing trust in small towns has required more than just showing up with equipment. Community outreach coordinators have worked with local churches, senior centers, and county health departments to spread awareness of the mobile clinic schedule and to reassure residents that the service is meant to complement, not replace, any existing relationships with local providers.

A CoxHealth outreach coordinator described the early response as encouraging but noted that consistency will be key to the program's long-term success. "You can't show up once and expect people to trust that this is real," she said. "We're committing to a regular schedule so people know exactly when we'll be back, and that predictability matters as much as the care itself."

## A Model for Rural Access

The mobile clinic initiative reflects a broader pattern among Missouri health systems experimenting with alternatives to traditional brick-and-mortar rural clinics, many of which have closed in recent years due to financial pressures and workforce shortages. CoxHealth officials say the mobile model allows them to test demand in underserved communities before committing to the far larger investment of a permanent clinic location.

Early data from the first several stops has shown steady turnout, according to hospital officials, though they caution it remains too soon to draw firm conclusions about long-term health impact. County health officials in the areas served by the program have welcomed the effort, noting that even modest increases in access to routine screening can help catch chronic conditions before they become medical emergencies requiring far more costly hospital care.
