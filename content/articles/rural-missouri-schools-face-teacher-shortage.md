School districts across rural Missouri continue to struggle with a persistent teacher shortage, a challenge that has prompted new recruitment and retention efforts from both state education officials and university teacher preparation programs in recent years.

The shortage is most acute in subject areas such as special education, mathematics, and science, where rural superintendents say they sometimes go through multiple hiring cycles without finding a qualified applicant. Several small districts have resorted to combining classes across grade levels or relying more heavily on long-term substitute teachers, a stopgap measure that education researchers generally caution can affect learning outcomes if sustained over multiple years.

## Why Rural Districts Struggle to Compete

Superintendents in rural parts of the state say they often struggle to compete with larger suburban and urban districts on salary, even though the cost of living in many rural Missouri communities is comparatively lower. Beyond pay, some new teachers cite concerns about limited housing options, fewer amenities, and professional isolation, since small schools may have only one or two teachers per subject area, offering less built-in mentorship or collaboration than a larger school might provide.

"It's not just about the paycheck, though that matters," said a rural district superintendent in northern Missouri. "New teachers sometimes worry about being the only person teaching their subject in the building, with nobody down the hall to collaborate with or ask for advice."

State education officials have responded with several initiatives, including loan forgiveness programs for teachers who commit to working in designated shortage areas, many of which are rural, and grow-your-own programs that encourage high school students from rural communities to pursue teaching degrees with the expectation that they will return to teach in their hometown districts after graduation.

## University Teacher Prep Programs Adjust Approach

Missouri's teacher preparation programs, including those at Truman State University, the University of Central Missouri, and several regional campuses, have adjusted recruitment strategies to specifically target students from rural backgrounds, on the theory that teachers who grew up in small towns are more likely to return to similar communities after earning their degree.

Some programs have also expanded rural student teaching placements, giving education majors direct classroom experience in small-town schools before graduation, an approach faculty say helps demystify rural teaching for students who might otherwise default toward more familiar suburban placements. Faculty coordinating these placements report that students who complete a rural student teaching assignment are more likely to consider a rural teaching job after graduation, even if they did not originally grow up in a small town themselves.

Despite these efforts, education officials caution that the shortage is unlikely to resolve quickly. Enrollment in teacher preparation programs nationally has declined over the past decade, a trend attributed to factors including relatively low starting salaries compared to other bachelor's degree career paths and public discourse around teacher working conditions. Rural superintendents say they remain hopeful that combined state, university, and local efforts will gradually ease the shortage, but most describe the current staffing situation as something they expect to keep managing carefully for at least the next several years.
